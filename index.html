02 / Our Mother

Our Mother

Mother Mercy has always had a gift for making people feel heard.

Raised in Natchez, Mississippi, she learned early that faith was something you practised: a place set at the table, a difficult conversation, a hand held steady when someone needed guidance.

Today, she offers that same care to those who find their way to Our Lady of Mercy. She listens patiently. She remembers what you tell her. And when the time comes to make a change, she will help you see it through.

You may arrive unsure of what you need. Mother will take the time to find out.

Wrestling

Her path to professional wrestling began on a ring crew. She helped put the ring together, watched the people who stepped inside it, and eventually trained to take her own place between the ropes. Along the way, she became someone others sought out for advice. Mercy listened carefully, offered direction, and expected people to follow through.

Now competing in HORIZON Pro Wrestling, Mother Mercy intends to make herself heard. Every opponent gives her someone new to reach. Every victory gives those watching another reason to listen. Every championship places her before a larger audience, and Mercy has never been careless with an audience’s attention.

She understands that some will resist her guidance. She is prepared to stay with them until they understand.

03 / What we believe

Correction is a form of care.

Everyone who comes to Our Lady of Mercy is welcomed with patience. Some arrive ready to listen. Others need time to understand why they have been brought here.

Mother makes room for both.

I.

Be known.

You may speak freely here. Tell Mother what troubles you, what you regret, and what you have never trusted anyone enough to say. She listens without interruption. She remembers, so that you will never have to begin again with someone else.

II.

Accept correction.

There may come a time when Mother asks something difficult of you. You may feel misunderstood. You may find yourself angry with her. Speak honestly about those feelings, then do what she has asked. Trust grows through practice.

III.

Remain in her care.

After a difficult lesson, it is natural to seek the comfort of familiar people. They may question what happened here. They may tell you that you have changed.

Give them time. Until then, bring your concerns to Mother. She knows how far you have come, and she will not allow anyone to undo your progress.

The camera sits at the far end of a kitchen table, propped slightly crooked.

Behind it, someone adjusts a chair.

There is a scrape against the floor, a pause, then Mercy Bell enters the frame. She wears a cream blouse with the sleeves rolled to her elbows. Her black leather coat hangs from a hook beside the door.

The room belongs to a church, or perhaps a community hall. Stacked chairs stand beneath a noticeboard covered in curling flyers. Behind Mercy, a handwritten sign above the sink reads: PLEASE LEAVE AS YOU FOUND IT.

She carries two mugs.

One she sets beside her own chair. The other she places near the camera, turning the handle towards whoever might sit there.

“There.”

She sits.

“I wasn’t sure how you took it.”

A small smile.

“So I left room.”

For a moment, she watches the empty place across from her. Somewhere beyond the kitchen, a heavy door closes.

“When I was little, my father used to hold people back after service. Everybody else would be outside, getting into their cars, deciding where to eat. And there’d be somebody sitting in the front pew with their head down.”

She cups her mug in both hands.

“I used to feel sorry for them. Missing their dinner. Knowing their family was out there waiting.”

“Then one Sunday, I saw a woman coming out of his office. Mrs. Hanley. She taught me how to make biscuits.”

Mercy glances towards the counter.

“She’d been crying so hard she couldn’t get her coat buttoned. I offered to help, and she caught my hand.”

Mercy closes her fingers around an absent wrist.

“She said, ‘Your daddy’s a good man.’”

“I hadn’t asked.”

Her grip loosens.

“People will tell you all sorts of things when they’re trying to make something bearable.”

She takes a drink. Sets the mug carefully inside an old ring on the tabletop.

“I’ve been waiting, Horizon. Watching.”

“I’ve been watching the people you’ve brought together. There’s plenty of footage if you know where to look.”

“You can learn a great deal about somebody from what they do after a match. Once the music stops. Once they’ve walked far enough up the aisle that they think the camera’s found something more interesting.”

“The little look towards the crowd. Checking whether somebody saw. The way a person pulls their arm away when somebody offers to help.”

She looks into the lens.

“I know that one.”

“I worked with a girl in Arkansas who’d do it every night. Hurt her shoulder in a match and wouldn’t let anybody near it. Could barely lift her bag.”

“So I carried it.”

Mercy smiles faintly at the memory.

“She cussed me all the way to the car.”

“The next morning, she asked if I’d help her tape it. By the end of the week, she was knocking on my door before breakfast.”

Her thumb travels slowly around the handle of her mug.

“Then she got a little better.”

“Started listening to people who hadn’t been there when she couldn’t get her own shirt on. Told me she needed some space.”

Mercy looks down.

“I’d given her my whole week.”

The refrigerator motor cuts out. In the sudden quiet, Mercy breathes through her nose.

“She said I was making her feel guilty.”

She considers that.

“Well.”

“If remembering what somebody did for you hurts, I don’t think forgetting it is the answer.”

Mercy reaches across the table and nudges the second mug closer to the empty chair.

“Some of you are going to dislike me.”

“I expect that. You’ve worked hard to become people who don’t need anything. You’ve got your names, your sayings. You know which face to make when somebody points a camera at you.”

“And here I come, asking how you’re sleeping.”

She gives a quiet, almost apologetic laugh.

“I can see how that might feel intrusive.”

“But sooner or later, somebody is going to put my name beside yours.”

Her eyes settle on the lens.

“And we’ll have a little time together.”

“I’ll take that arm you keep favouring. I’ll feel where you’ve learned to protect it. Where you’ve been hurt before.”

“You’ll reach for the ropes. You should. I’d be disappointed if you didn’t try.”

She shifts in her chair, leaning forward slightly.

“But when I bring you back into the middle, I want you to listen.”

“You’ll hear people telling you to hold on. People who get to go home with both shoulders working. They’ll ask you for another second, then another, because it costs them nothing.”

“I’ll be right there.”

Her voice softens.

“I’ll know exactly what it’s costing you.”

Mercy lifts one hand, flexing her fingers as though easing stiffness from them.

“You can be angry afterwards. You can say things about me. I won’t hold you to anything you say while you’re hurting.”

“But you’ll remember that I gave you a way to make it stop.”

There are footsteps outside.

Mercy turns towards the doorway. An older man in a caretaker’s fleece appears, keys in his hand.

“Sorry, love. We’re closing up.”

“Of course.”

Her smile comes easily.

“Just finishing.”

He notices the second cup.

“Someone coming?”

Mercy looks at the empty chair.

“I thought so.”

“Well. Five minutes, all right?”

“Thank you.”

He leaves. She waits until his footsteps fade before turning back. She picks up the untouched mug and carries it to the sink. The coffee pours away in a thin, steady stream.

“You don’t owe me your whole story yet.”

She rinses the cup.

“Just come and introduce yourself.”

Mercy dries it carefully, returns it to the table and turns the handle towards the empty chair again.

“I’d rather hear it from you.”

She reaches towards the camera.

The recording ends.

Addressed to HARUKO

The community room is almost dark. One fluorescent strip remains lit above the kitchenette. Its reflection lies across the nearest folding table, stopping short of the chairs stacked against the opposite wall.

Mercy Bell sits beneath it.

Her black leather coat is folded beside a small travel bag. A larger case stands by the door, its handle extended. On the table are a bus ticket, a paperback Bible, folded ring gear and a wash bag.

She checks the ticket once more before slipping it into the front pocket of her bag.

“Philadelphia.”

She says it quietly, trying the word.

“I like knowing where I’m going.”

Mercy smooths the folded coat with her palm.

“My father used to keep our bags packed. Even when we had somewhere to stay for a few weeks. Said it was good practice to be ready when you were called. I got very good at leaving things behind.”

Her hand stops.

“People, too.”

She looks towards the camera.

“I’ve been looking forward to meeting you, HARUKO. I’ve watched what I could find. You don’t seem to need much persuading when there’s an opportunity to make things hurt. I know that takes experience. There are things you’ve learned to endure that would send somebody else looking for the door.”

A small nod.

“What interests me is why you keep going back.”

Mercy opens the travel bag and checks the inside pocket.

“I read about StarCat. MAPLE. Mikasa. Quite a life to fit into a few paragraphs.”

“It always looks tidy when somebody writes it down. You went here. You did this. Things changed, so you went somewhere else.”

She glances at the case by the door.

“They leave out the packing. The little decisions. Whether something’s worth taking. Whether you ought to leave a note. How long you can stand in a doorway before somebody asks what you’re waiting for.”

Mercy leans back.

“I don’t know what was said when Mikasa got her opportunity. I wasn’t there.”

“Perhaps you were happy for her.”

She allows a moment.

“You can be happy for somebody and still have to go home alone and live with it afterwards.”

Beyond the kitchenette, a pipe knocks inside the wall.

“You probably decided the only person who can help HARUKO is HARUKO. You’re entitled to that.”

Her fingers close around the zip.

“And I’m entitled to wonder who taught you.”

She opens the wash bag and checks the cap on a small bottle of soap.

“You found people to travel with. People to work with. Someone who helped you bring out a part of yourself you’d kept put away. And now you’re here, making sure everybody understands how dangerous you are.”

“I believe you, sweetheart.”

She wipes a small trace of soap from the bottle cap with her thumb and closes the wash bag.

“I don’t need you to put yourself through anything on my account. You’ve already done enough to convince me you can take pain. What troubles me is how much of your life you seem willing to spend proving it.”

Mercy puts the wash bag into the case beside her boots.

“There’s always somebody willing to watch. They’ll love how hard you hit. How far you’ll fall. How you keep getting up. And when you’re tired, they’ll tell you that’s what makes you special.”

She looks directly into the lens.

“Be careful who benefits from that.”

For a few seconds, she holds the camera’s gaze. Then she lifts the folded ring gear from the table.

“I know you enjoy it, HARUKO. I’m not going to explain your own pleasure to you. You’ve learned it. Made choices about it. You’ve gone looking for things other people advised you to leave alone.”

“But I wonder what an ordinary match leaves unfinished. You can beat somebody with your hands. You’ve had the training. So what does the chair give you?”

“Does it make the victory feel more certain?”

She smooths a crease in the ivory fabric.

“Or does it make certain they’ll remember who they were in there with? I can understand wanting that. Especially when people have found it so easy to move on.”

Mercy folds the top once more and lays it inside the case.

“Perhaps you simply like it. You’re allowed to tell me that. But I won’t agree to leave it alone just because you’ve found something you enjoy. My father enjoyed having a room full of people listen to him. I still had to live with what he did.”

The zip catches. She frees a strand of fabric and draws it closed.

“I’ve made time for you. I’m packing my life into those bags and coming to Philadelphia.”

A faint smile.

“You don’t owe me gratitude. But I would appreciate you taking it seriously.”

She lowers the case onto its wheels.

“I’m not coming to Philadelphia expecting you to be frightened of pain. That would be foolish.”

“I think you’ll recognise quite a lot of what I do. The arm held a little longer than it needs to be. The elbow finding somewhere tender.”

“You might even appreciate it.”

She turns the case towards the door.

“I won’t need a chair to make an impression on you.”

“I’ll take what you give me. A wrist. A chance to get behind you. I’ll make you work for the distance.”

Mercy picks up her coat.

“I’ve wrestled in rooms where people couldn’t see what was happening in front of their eyes. You could spend ten minutes taking somebody apart limb by limb and they’d still ask when the match was going to get going.”

“Bring out a table, though.”

She shakes her head faintly.

“Everybody understood that. Then they got excited.”

“I expect you know the difference between what makes people watch and what makes a person hurt.”

Mercy slips one arm into the coat.

“I’m more interested in the second.”

She draws the coat over her other shoulder.

“And when I get Absolution secured, I won’t be looking out at the people watching us. I’ll be looking at you. I’ll have my head beside yours.”

“You can tell me something then without having to make it part of the performance.”

A small pause.

“That’s an offer, HARUKO.”

“I’d like there to be one moment that evening when you don’t feel obliged to be impressive.”

Mercy adjusts her collar.

“You may have no interest in giving me that. You may want to hurt me badly enough that I stop asking.”

She looks directly into the camera.

“I won’t give up on you, though, HARUKO. Not like the others.”

Mercy lifts the Bible from the table and places it inside her bag.

“I’d prefer we had a conversation before Philadelphia.”

“Somewhere you could leave whenever you wanted.”

Her hand rests on the open bag.

“But the ring will have to do.”

She closes the zip.

“And SULTANA.”

“I haven’t overlooked you. I’ve been thinking about you as well.”

She lifts the bag onto her shoulder.

“Your family seems to have made some very important decisions about your life.”

“I’d like to know which ones you were allowed to make yourself.”

At the door, she takes the handle of her case.

“We’ll speak about that next.”

A small, reassuring smile.

“We already have an appointment.”

She switches off the light.

Addressed to SULTANA

The statue stands behind a low wooden rail, its painted hands extended towards the empty hall. Someone has arranged artificial flowers at its feet. In the weak light beneath the ceiling tiles, their white petals have taken on a greyish blue.

Mercy Bell kneels on a narrow cushion before it.

Her black leather coat rests on the bench behind her. She wears a cream blouse, buttoned at the throat, its sleeves drawn down over her wrists. Her hands are clasped beneath her chin.

For several seconds, there is only the sound of ventilation.

“Help me find the right words.”

Her head remains bowed.

“And the patience to let them be heard.”

She opens her eyes.

“My father used to introduce me before I sang. He’d put his hand between my shoulders and tell everybody what a blessing I was. Such a good girl. Such a willing heart.”

Mercy lowers her hands into her lap.

“Sometimes that would be the first I’d heard that I was singing. I might have a sore throat. I might not know the song he’d chosen. But everybody would already be looking at me.”

“And he’d already told them I was willing.”

She rubs her thumb across the base of one finger.

“So I’d get up.”

“Afterwards, people would tell him how proud he must be. I’d stand beside him while they said it.”

Her eyes settle on the statue.

“I learned quite young that there were things I could do with my own life which would be considered something I’d done to him.”

“Embarrass him. Disappoint him. Make him look like a man who couldn’t manage his own daughter.”

A small movement at the corner of her mouth.

“He was very particular about that.”

Mercy turns her head towards the camera.

“SULTANA.”

“I’ve been thinking about what you’ve inherited. Your family’s history. Your uncle’s instruction. The promises that have survived long enough to reach you. There must have been a great deal to learn.”

She shifts slightly on the cushion.

“And I expect there were people pleased to see you learning it. Youngest daughter. Last surviving family.”

Mercy allows the words to sit in the room.

“That’s a great deal of importance to place on somebody before she’s had a chance to speak. I wonder what would have happened if you’d wanted something else. Would they have listened? Would they have asked you to think about it a little longer?”

She looks down at her hands.

“Would somebody have reminded you what had been sacrificed so you could be there?”

The ventilation stops. Her next breath is audible.

“I’m asking because I know how reasonable it can sound. Your uncle gave you his time. He trained you. There are things you can do now because somebody took the trouble to teach you.”

“Of course you’re grateful. But gratitude is a useful thing to have from a child. You can keep asking for more long after she’s grown.”

Mercy lifts her gaze.

“I don’t know what your family said to you in private. I won’t pretend I do. But I know what it means to be handed a purpose by somebody who expects to benefit from your devotion.”

“And I’d like you to consider whether the people who made those promises ever expected to be the ones paying for them.”

She glances towards the flowers.

“My father believed I’d been given to him for a reason. He’d say that when I was being difficult. Imagine being told your existence is a gift, then finding out the person who received it considers you his to use.”

Mercy reaches forward and straightens a flower stem protruding through the rail.

“I wouldn’t ask you to explain your whole family to me. You could begin with something small. Something you were expected to do.”

“Something you didn’t want.”

Her fingers remain around the stem.

“You might find it difficult to say. That wouldn’t trouble me. I’m prepared to be patient.”

She releases it.

“But I would expect you to be honest.”

A pause.

“There’s very little I can do for someone who insists on protecting the people who taught her to suffer.”

Mercy places one hand on the rail and rises. She takes a moment to straighten her skirt before sitting on the bench beside her coat.

“You’re strong, SULTANA. There’s nothing uncertain about the way you put someone down. Violence exists in your blood.”

“I respect that.”

She rolls one shoulder slowly.

“And I’ve paid attention to CRUCIFICA. The impact would be quite enough for most people. But you stay with her. You take the arms. You make certain she understands there’s more.”

Mercy’s hands settle on her knees.

“I would be careless to let you take both my arms.”

“Knowing a hold doesn’t mean you’ll always escape it.”

“So when you try to lift me, I’ll be making you work for it.”

She presses a thumb into the opposite palm.

“And when you reach for me, I’ll be looking for the arm you’ve offered. I intend to make you work in turn.”

“I will keep your shoulder hurting, and you’ll have to account for it. Every time.”

Mercy looks towards the camera.

“You may be stronger than me, SULTANA. I don’t need to be prideful about that.”

“I need to be thorough.”

She draws her coat into her lap.

“You’ll try to hurt me. You may succeed. I accept this.”

“But when I get behind you, when I’ve taken your arm and brought you down, I’ll be asking you to make a decision for yourself. Nobody in your family can make it for you.”

“Whatever was promised, whatever you were taught to endure, you’ll be the one feeling it.”

Her voice softens.

“And you’ll have a way to make it stop.”

Mercy begins folding the coat along its seams.

“You might hate me for putting you in that position. I understand. It can be humiliating to discover how much pain you’ve accepted because you didn’t feel entitled to refuse it.”

“But when you finally ask me to let go, I’ll know you’ve heard something I’ve been trying to tell you.”

She smooths a sleeve.

“You’re allowed to want relief. You’re allowed to disappoint people.”

Her hand stills.

“You’re allowed to listen to someone who has taken the time to understand what’s happening to you.”

For a moment, she watches the lens as though waiting for an answer.

“I won’t insist that you thank me straight away. But I would like you to remember who was there when all those old promises became more than you could bear.”

Mercy stands with the coat over one arm. She looks back towards the statue, then kneels once more. Her hands come together.

“I’ve given this thought, SULTANA. I’ve prayed about it. I’m willing to take the anger that comes with helping you.”

She bows her head.

“But I won’t keep explaining myself while you make the same mistakes.”

Her eyes close.

“When I tell you to be still, I’ll need you to be still. When I tell you that you’ve had enough, you’ll listen.”

A quiet breath.

“You’ve been obedient for people who were never going to release you.”

Mercy raises her head towards the camera.

“You can be obedient for me long enough to learn the difference.”
